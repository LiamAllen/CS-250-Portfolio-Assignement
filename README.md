## Questions

### Q-1: How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

Implementing user needs into a program is a fairly straighforward   process. First, the users' needs are evaluated. By speaking with the stakeholders of the project and gathering information from the end-users themselves, a product owner can define user stories that address each one of these needs. User stories make implementing defined requirements much easier as they provide the developer with a guide to what users need to be able to acheive, why they need to acheive it, and which users should be able to achieve it. Having a straightforward guide such as this helps teams of multiple developers stay on the same page regarding the details of a requirement as well as allowing developers to have a more complete understanding of each requirement.

### Q-2: How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

When I began developing, I held a very "head-first" approach to my projects. Instead of utilizing effective planning methods, I threw myself into the project and began writing code. While this may be an effective practice in small homelab projects, in an enterprise setting, this behavior can lead to a variety of problems. One practice that I have employed successfully in my internship is the iterative development process. By utilizing iterative development, I can frequently receive feedback regarding requirements I have completed as well as how I completed it. 

### Q-3: What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member means taking part in the collective ownership of a project, leaving no work item or issue as "somebody else's problem". A good team member will adapt to work as a unit with their peers to ensure that their responsibilities are met as well as their teammates'. Furthermore, good team members will do their part to ensure that issues such as silod knowledge, technical dept, and scope creep are either mitigated or avoided.
